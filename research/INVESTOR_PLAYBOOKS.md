# Investor playbooks: what the best actually do, and what the Scout copies

Research date: 2026-11-13. Sources are listed at the end. This file answers one question: which approach gives a small investor starting near zero the best odds of real wealth without a wipeout?

## The short answer

**Few deep convictions, in places big money cannot go, on top of a rising amount invested.** Not "many small plays and hope one hits."

Three findings drive this.

1. **Winners are rare, so random picking loses.** Across roughly 29,000 US stocks since 1926, under 4% produced all of the market's net gain over Treasury bills, and the typical stock lost money over its life (Bessembinder, updated 2026). A handful of ordinary picks is more likely to miss the winners than to hold one.
2. **The investors with the best records were concentrated, small and patient.** Buffett's partnership (1957 to 1969) compounded about 29.5% a year before fees and put up to 40% in a single idea. Greenblatt's Gotham (1985 to 1994) made about 50% a year before fees with six to eight positions making up most of the portfolio. Both worked in small, ignored or forced-selling situations, and both shut or shrank when they got too big.
3. **Effort is the edge an individual can actually have.** In the largest study of angel exits, deals with more than 20 hours of checking returned 5.9 times the money, against 1.1 times for deals with less. Households that concentrated in a few stocks beat diversified ones by 1 to 4 points a year, mostly in local and smaller companies, and only for accounts above $25,000.

## Playbooks by asset type

| Area | Who did it best | What they look for | Entry | Exit | Honest odds |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Public stocks, small and special situations | Buffett partnership, Greenblatt, Lynch | Spin-offs, forced sellers, scandal-hit good businesses, companies too small for funds | After the forced selling, when price is far under a conservative value | Value reached, or thesis broken | Best fit for a small account. Still hard: most stocks lose |
| Insider buying | Academic record, 1986 to 2016 | Several insiders buying the same small company within days | After the filings, small companies only | 3 to 12 months | A modest edge (about 3.8% extra in a month for cluster buys); a screen, not a strategy |
| Startups and private companies | Angel groups | Founders and markets they know from the inside | Early rounds, after 20 to 40+ hours of checking | Acquisition or listing, 3 to 6 years | 52% of deals lose money; 7% make 75% of all returns; needs 20+ deals and usually accredited status |
| Buying a small business | Search-fund operators | Dull, profitable firms with an owner who wants out | Purchase with investor backing; the buyer runs it | Sale after 5 to 8 years | 35% a year pooled return, but about 4 in 10 searches never buy and about 3 in 10 purchases lose money |
| Real estate | Small landlords | Owner-occupied multi-unit, bought with a low down payment | When rent covers the payment with a margin | Hold, refinance | Returns come from borrowing; no strong independent data found; rent-versus-buy math must clear first |
| Commodities and futures | Trend followers | Price already moving, across many markets at once | Buy strength, sell weakness, by rule | When the trend reverses | Works as a rule-based system over a century; a diversifier, long flat stretches, not a wealth engine |
| Crypto | Early, large holders | Bought before the crowd | Before adoption, not after a run | Sold into retail buying | The median retail buyer of bitcoin from 2015 to 2022 lost about half; most bought after prices rose |

## How people actually go from near zero to a million

- At the top, wealth is mostly **business ownership and company shares**. For the bottom 90% it is pensions and housing (Smith, Zidar and Zwick).
- Self-made millionaires most often name investments, pay, and **employee stock or profit sharing** as the source (Fidelity).
- The arithmetic at small sizes: with $1,500 a month invested and rising 5% a year, reaching $1 million in 10 years needs about **30% a year**. That is Buffett-partnership level. In 15 years it needs about 13%.
- Raising the amount invested does more than raising the return. The same saver stepping up to $3,000 a month in year 4 and $5,000 in year 7 reaches about $560,000 in 10 years at a plain 7%, against $314,000 without the step-ups.

So the fastest realistic route has three engines, in this order of importance early on:

1. **Earn and own.** Income growth, equity in an employer, or a business the owner builds. This is the Scout's build-play area and it matters most while the portfolio is small.
2. **Concentrate where there is an edge.** A few deep positions in small, ignored or special situations, each backed by a full dossier.
3. **Keep the base compounding.** Everything not in a play sits in the whole market, which always holds the rare winners.

## What changes in the Scout

1. **Model: few deep convictions.** At most six open money plays at once. No play without a named source of edge.
2. **Edge test.** Every play must say why the price is wrong and why professionals have not fixed it: too small, forced selling, complexity, a dated catalyst, or work the owner can do that others will not.
3. **Diligence bar.** A dossier is required before any sized bet, covering filings, insiders, debt, customers and the bear case.
4. **Where the Radar looks first.** Spin-off filings (Form 10), clusters of insider purchases (Form 4), companies leaving bankruptcy, tender offers and rights offerings, businesses for sale, and build plays.
5. **What stays out.** Crypto bought after a run, trend trading by hand, startup investing without the deal count or status to do it properly.
6. **Sizing stays honest.** Bets come from the calculator and the trust ladder. Bigger bets are earned by a graded record, not by wanting them.

## Limits of this research

- The famous records are survivors. For every Buffett there were many concentrated investors who failed and are not written about.
- Buffett and Greenblatt worked in a less crowded market. The same situations are more closely watched today.
- The angel and search-fund figures come from people who answered surveys, which flatters results.
- None of this proves the Scout has skill. Only its own graded forecasts can.
